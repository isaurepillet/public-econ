\noindent \textbf{Research Question}: What are the effects of automatic public intermediation of child support on payment compliance, on the economic security of creditor parents, and on the behaviour of debtor parents ? \\

\subsection{Rossin-Slater and Wüst (2018): Parental Responses to Child Support Obligations}

\paragraph{Research Question}
The authors investigate how child support obligations affect parental behaviour following separation. Specifically, they examine whether higher mandated payments increase actual financial transfers to children and how parents adjust their custody arrangements, subsequent fertility, and labour market behaviour.

\paragraph{Data}
The study uses Danish administrative register data covering children born between 1985 and 2010, linked to their biological parents regardless of residential arrangements. The registers provide annual information on income, employment, earnings, marital and cohabitation status, educational attainment, family composition, and tax-reported child support payments.

The main analysis covers separations occurring between 1999 and 2010, with outcomes observed through 2013. The final sample contains 40,109 fathers, each linked to the mother and children. The authors restrict the sample to fathers with one or two children under 18 and separation-year gross incomes between DKK 270,000 and 405,000, expressed in 2000 prices.

A limitation is that the administrative records do not identify formal versus informal child support agreements, fully observe shared custody, or measure in-kind transfers and visits.

\paragraph{Identification Strategy}
The authors exploit quasi-exogenous variation generated by the Danish child support formula. Mandated obligations are a nonlinear step function of the father's gross income, the number of children, and the calendar year. The income thresholds and payment amounts change over time, generating differential exposure across otherwise comparable fathers.

The empirical strategy uses a \textbf{simulated instrumental variables (SIV)} approach. The potentially endogenous explanatory variable is the father's average annual child support obligation over the post-separation period, since subsequent income and fertility may themselves respond to child support obligations.

The authors instrument this variable using the statutory obligation calculated exclusively from the father's income, number of children, and applicable formula in the year of separation:

\[
Z_i=g_{t_i}(I_{i,t_i},K_{i,t_i}).
\]

The first stage is:
\[
\overline{Obligation}_i
=\alpha+\pi Z_i+\boldsymbol{\gamma}'X_i
+\mathcal{F}_i+u_i,
\]
and the second stage is:
\[
Y_i=\beta_0+
\beta_1\widehat{\overline{Obligation}}_i+
\boldsymbol{\delta}'X_i+\mathcal{F}_i+\varepsilon_i.
\]

Here, $Y_i$ represents a post-separation outcome, $X_i$ includes parental and child characteristics measured at separation, and $\mathcal{F}_i$ denotes fixed effects for separation year, number of children, and DKK 15,000 income bins, together with all pairwise interactions between these three dimensions. The specification also controls for the number of original children remaining under 18 in subsequent years.

Identification therefore comes from the residual variation in statutory obligations across income, family size, and separation year, conditional on their main effects and pairwise interactions. The design resembles a triple-difference strategy.

The main identifying assumption is that this residual formula-driven variation is uncorrelated with unobserved determinants of subsequent parental behaviour. The authors investigate selection into separation, balance in predetermined parental characteristics, strategic sorting around income thresholds, and pre-separation labour supply. They find no systematic evidence undermining the identifying assumption. The preferred first-stage F-statistic is approximately 94.

\paragraph{Main Results}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Child support payments:} A DKK 1,000 increase in average annual mandated obligations increases observed average annual payments by only DKK 273 (significant at the 10\% level). Payments therefore respond substantially less than one-for-one to higher obligations.

    \item \textbf{Voluntary transfers:} The probability of paying more than the mandated obligation falls by approximately 2.3 percentage points (significant at the 1\% level), consistent with partial crowding out of voluntary payments.

    \item \textbf{Father-child co-residence:} A DKK 1,000 increase in average annual obligations reduces the probability that the father lives with his child in at least one post-separation year by approximately 1.8 percentage points (significant at the 5\% level), around 6.5\% relative to the sample mean.

    \item \textbf{Subsequent fertility:} Higher obligations increase the probability that fathers have another child while married to a new partner by approximately 1.2 percentage points (significant at the 1\% level). The authors find no statistically significant effect on fathers' overall subsequent fertility or mothers' subsequent fertility.

    \item \textbf{Labour market outcomes:} The authors find no statistically significant effects on fathers' or mothers' labour market participation or earnings.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation and Limitations}
The findings suggest that child support mandates generate behavioural adjustments beyond monetary payments. Higher obligations increase observed transfers, but may crowd out voluntary financial contributions and are associated with reduced father-child co-residence. The latter is consistent with substitution between financial and non-financial parental investments, although the data cannot directly identify the underlying mechanism.

The less-than-one-for-one payment response cannot be interpreted solely as non-compliance: informal agreements, unobserved shared custody arrangements, and voluntary payments also contribute to the gap between statutory obligations and observed payments. Furthermore, the co-residence measure does not capture all forms of contact between fathers and children.

Because the sample focuses on relatively higher-income fathers, the results may not generalise to lower-income families or other institutional contexts.

\paragraph{Relevance for Our Research Project}
This paper provides an important benchmark for studying behavioural responses to child support policies using linked administrative data. It demonstrates that interventions affecting child support can influence both monetary transfers and non-monetary parental investments.

However, the policy variation differs from our proposed evaluation of France's 2022--2023 reform. Rossin-Slater and Wüst identify the effects of changes in the \emph{amount of statutory child support obligations}, whereas the French reform changes the \emph{administration and enforcement of payments}, without mechanically increasing the amounts owed.

Our project would therefore complement their analysis by examining whether automatic public intermediation improves actual payment compliance, the financial security of creditor parents, and potentially debtor behaviour. In particular, their results motivate investigating whether more effective payment enforcement generates behavioural responses beyond changes in financial transfers.


\subsection{Cancian, Heinrich and Chung (2013):
Discouraging Disadvantaged Fathers' Employment:
An Unintended Consequence of Policies Designed to Support Families}

\paragraph{Research Question}

The authors investigate whether child support-related
debt discourages formal employment and reduces child
support payments among economically disadvantaged fathers.

More specifically, they examine the causal effects of
additional debt arising from Medicaid-financed childbirth
expenses on fathers' subsequent formal earnings and
financial contributions to their children.

The central question is whether policies intended to
recover public expenditures may generate unintended
labour supply responses, thereby undermining the
financial support available to children.

\paragraph{Institutional Context}

The study focuses on Wisconsin, United States,
where unmarried fathers may be required to reimburse
childbirth expenses initially covered by Medicaid.

These birth-related obligations are imposed in addition
to regular child support obligations.

For fathers with limited financial resources, such
charges may create substantial debt early in the
child's life.

Because child support obligations can be collected
through wage withholding, additional debt may reduce
the effective financial return to formal employment.

Two competing mechanisms are possible.

First, an income effect may encourage fathers to
increase their labour supply to meet additional
financial obligations.

Second, a substitution or discouragement effect
may induce fathers to reduce formal employment
when a substantial share of their earnings is
subject to collection.

The direction of the response may therefore depend
on fathers' previous labour market attachment.

\paragraph{Data}

The authors construct a longitudinal administrative
dataset linking records from Wisconsin's child
support enforcement system (KIDS) with
Unemployment Insurance earnings records.

The final sample contains 8,263 fathers whose
children were born between 1998 and 2003.

The monetary data cover observations between
1997 and 2005.

The analysis focuses on fathers subject to both
regular child support obligations and orders
to reimburse Medicaid childbirth expenses.

The baseline is defined as the first full quarter
after both a positive child support obligation
and a birth-cost reimbursement order are observed.

The authors follow fathers during the first
two years after baseline.

The principal outcomes are annual child support
payments and formal earnings reported through
the Unemployment Insurance system.

The main explanatory variable is child support
debt, while the identification strategy relies
on typical childbirth costs assigned at the
county-month level.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{Descriptive statistics (Table 1):}

\begin{itemize}

    \item Average actual childbirth costs:
    \$2,265.

    \item Average typical childbirth costs:
    \$2,305.

    \item Average child support debt at baseline:
    \$2,370.

    \item Average child support debt at the
    beginning of the second year:
    \$3,018.

    \item Share of fathers making at least
    one child support payment:
    77.2\% in year 1 and 72.1\% in year 2.

    \item Average annual child support payments:
    \$1,539 in year 1 and \$1,569 in year 2.

    \item Share of fathers with positive
    formal earnings:
    74.5\% in year 1 and 69.9\% in year 2.

    \item Average annual formal earnings:
    \$9,256 in year 1 and \$9,613 in year 2.

\end{itemize}

These statistics illustrate the substantial
financial burden of childbirth-related debt
relative to fathers' earnings.

An important data limitation is that
Unemployment Insurance records do not
fully capture informal employment,
self-employment, or unreported earnings.

\paragraph{Identification Strategy}

\textbf{Endogeneity problem.}

A simple regression of employment or payments
on child support debt would not necessarily
identify a causal effect.

Fathers with weak labour market attachment
may accumulate greater debt because they
have fewer resources to meet their obligations.

Conversely, higher debt may itself discourage
formal employment.

This creates a potential reverse-causality
problem, together with omitted-variable bias
from unobserved characteristics affecting
both debt and labour market outcomes.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{Instrumental variables approach.}

The authors exploit variation in typical
Medicaid childbirth costs across Wisconsin
counties and months.

These typical costs are determined by local
administrative and medical charging practices,
rather than directly by individual fathers'
employment decisions.

They are strongly correlated with the
birth-related debt assigned to fathers.

The authors therefore use typical childbirth
costs as an instrument for actual child
support debt.

The empirical strategy can be summarised
by the following two-stage framework.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{First stage:}

\begin{equation}
D_i
=
\pi_0
+
\pi_1 Z_{c(i),m(i)}
+
X_i'\pi_2
+
W_{c(i)}'\pi_3
+
u_i,
\end{equation}

where $D_i$ denotes father $i$'s child
support debt, and $Z_{c(i),m(i)}$ is
the typical childbirth cost in the
relevant county and month.

$X_i$ includes individual characteristics
and pre-birth labour market histories.

$W_{c(i)}$ represents county-level
labour market controls, including
employment shares and average earnings
across industries.

The models also include county indicators
and other observed characteristics.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{Second stage:}

\begin{equation}
Y_{it}
=
\alpha_t
+
\beta_t \widehat{D}_i
+
\sum_{k=1}^{3}
\gamma_{kt} E_{ik}
+
\sum_{k=1}^{3}
\delta_{kt}
\left(
\widehat{D_i E_{ik}}
\right)
+
X_i'\theta_t
+
W_{c(i)}'\lambda_t
+
\varepsilon_{it},
\end{equation}

where $Y_{it}$ denotes either child
support payments or formal earnings
in year $t$ after baseline.

$E_{ik}$ identifies the father's
pre-birth employment category.

The reference category consists of
fathers with no formal earnings
during the 7--18 months before
the child's birth.

The three additional categories are:

\begin{itemize}

    \item Employment during one to
    three quarters.

    \item Employment during all four
    quarters with a single employer.

    \item Employment during all four
    quarters with multiple employers.

\end{itemize}

The interaction terms allow the effect
of additional debt to differ according
to previous labour market attachment.

In the IV specification, both debt
and its interactions with employment
history are treated as endogenous.

The corresponding instruments include
typical childbirth costs and their
interactions with pre-birth employment
categories.

The equations above are simplified
representations of the authors'
empirical strategy.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{Identification assumptions.}

The relevance condition requires typical
childbirth costs to predict actual debt.

The exclusion restriction requires
variation in typical childbirth costs
not to affect subsequent payments or
earnings through channels other than
the resulting debt, conditional on controls.

This assumption would be threatened
if county-month childbirth costs
were correlated with unobserved local
conditions directly influencing
fathers' employment.

\paragraph{First-Stage Results}

The first-stage estimates confirm a
strong positive relationship between
typical childbirth costs and actual
child support debt.

According to Appendix Table C1,
a \$1,000 increase in typical childbirth
costs is associated with:

\begin{itemize}

    \item A \$707 increase in child
    support debt at baseline
    ($p<0.01$).

    \item A \$529 increase in child
    support debt one year after
    baseline ($p<0.01$).

\end{itemize}

These estimates support the relevance
of the instrumental variable.

However, a strong first stage alone
does not establish the validity
of the exclusion restriction.

\paragraph{Main Results}

\textbf{1. Effects on child support payments
(Table 2).}

The IV estimates reveal substantial
heterogeneity according to fathers'
pre-birth employment histories.

For fathers with no formal earnings
before the child's birth, an additional
\$1,000 of debt is associated with:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \$126 higher annual child
    support payments in year 1.

    \item \$144 higher annual child
    support payments in year 2.

\end{itemize}

Neither estimate is statistically
significant.

For fathers with previous employment,
the interaction coefficients are
negative and statistically significant.

In particular, for an additional
\$1,000 of debt, relative to fathers
with no prior employment:

\begin{itemize}

    \item Fathers employed during
    one to three quarters:
    interaction effects of
    $-\$268$ in year 1 and
    $-\$234$ in year 2
    (both $p<0.01$).

    \item Fathers employed during
    all four quarters with one
    employer:
    interaction effects of
    $-\$357$ in year 1 and
    $-\$374$ in year 2
    (both $p<0.01$).

    \item Fathers employed during
    all four quarters with multiple
    employers:
    interaction effects of
    $-\$316$ in year 1 and
    $-\$312$ in year 2
    (both $p<0.01$).

\end{itemize}

Importantly, these interaction
coefficients are differential effects,
not total marginal effects.

For example, for fathers previously
employed during all four quarters
with one employer, the implied
point estimate of the total effect
in year 1 is:

\begin{equation}
126 - 357 = -231.
\end{equation}

Thus, an additional \$1,000 of
instrumented debt is associated
with approximately \$231 less
in annual child support payments
for this group in year 1.

This calculation combines the
main debt coefficient and the
relevant interaction coefficient.

The statistical significance of
the combined marginal effect
cannot be inferred from the
individual significance stars alone.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{2. Effects on formal earnings
(Table 3).}

The authors also find heterogeneous
effects of debt on formal earnings.

For fathers with no formal earnings
before childbirth, the IV point
estimates are positive:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \$240 in year 1.

    \item \$437 in year 2.

\end{itemize}

However, these coefficients are
not statistically significant.

For fathers previously employed
during all four quarters with
multiple employers, the interaction
effects are substantially negative.

An additional \$1,000 of
instrumented debt is associated
with differential earnings effects of:

\begin{itemize}

    \item $-\$584$ in year 1
    ($p<0.05$).

    \item $-\$851$ in year 2
    ($p<0.05$).

\end{itemize}

For fathers previously employed
during all four quarters with
a single employer, the corresponding
interaction effects are:

\begin{itemize}

    \item $-\$436$ in year 1.

    \item $-\$728$ in year 2.

\end{itemize}

These latter IV interaction
coefficients are not statistically
significant at conventional levels.

The authors interpret the negative
interactions as evidence that
additional debt can discourage
formal labour market activity
among fathers who were previously
more strongly attached to employment.

For fathers with multiple employers,
the negative response may partly
reflect reductions in supplementary
work effort.

\medskip

\noindent
\textbf{3. Heterogeneous responses.}

The results suggest that the
labour market consequences of
child support debt depend on
fathers' ability to adjust
their working hours and earnings.

Fathers with little previous
employment may respond to
additional obligations by
attempting to increase earnings.

By contrast, fathers already
working regularly may face
limited opportunities to
increase labour supply.

For these fathers, the reduced
effective return to additional
formal earnings may generate
a discouragement effect.

\paragraph{Interpretation and Limitations}

The findings suggest that
additional child support debt
can produce unintended effects
on fathers' economic behaviour.

Rather than mechanically
increasing financial transfers,
larger obligations may reduce
formal earnings and current
child support payments among
previously employed fathers.

Several limitations are important.

First, the instrumental variables
strategy relies on an exclusion
restriction that cannot be
directly tested.

Second, the analysis measures
formal earnings rather than
total labour income.

Observed reductions may therefore
partly reflect substitution
towards informal or unreported work.

Third, the findings concern
Medicaid childbirth-cost
reimbursement obligations
in Wisconsin, rather than
ordinary changes in monthly
child support amounts.

Finally, the results are
heterogeneous: it would be
misleading to interpret the
negative interaction coefficients
as evidence that all fathers
reduce employment or payments
when debt increases.

\paragraph{Relevance for Our Research Project}

This paper provides an important
theoretical and empirical motivation
for investigating debtor responses
to France's 2022--2023 reform
introducing automatic intermediation
of child support payments.

The authors show that policies
intended to improve financial
support for children can
generate behavioural responses
among non-resident fathers.

In particular, their findings
highlight the possibility that
stronger collection mechanisms
may affect incentives to
participate in formal employment.

However, the institutional
mechanism differs from
the French reform.

Cancian et al.\ examine the
effects of additional debt
arising from childbirth costs,
whereas automatic intermediation
primarily changes how existing
child support obligations
are administered and collected.

Our project could therefore
investigate whether improved
payment collection increases
the financial resources
received by creditor parents
without generating adverse
employment responses among
debtor parents.

The paper also strongly motivates
an analysis of heterogeneous
treatment effects by pre-reform
employment history, income,
and financial vulnerability.

More generally, it suggests
that the success of child
support policies should be
evaluated not only through
payment compliance but also
through their potential effects
on labour market behaviour
and families' economic resources.


\subsection{Aizer and McLanahan (2006):
The Impact of Child Support Enforcement on Fertility,
Parental Investments, and Child Well-Being}

\paragraph{Research Question}

The authors investigate whether stronger child support
enforcement (CSE) affects nonmarital fertility, parental
investments, and children's health outcomes.

The central hypothesis is that stricter enforcement
increases the expected financial cost of nonmarital
fatherhood, thereby affecting fertility decisions.

The authors distinguish two potential channels:
(i) increased financial resources available to children,
and (ii) changes in the composition of parents who
have children, potentially favouring parents with
a greater propensity to invest in child well-being.

\paragraph{Institutional Context}

The study examines the United States, where child support
enforcement was substantially strengthened during the
1980s and 1990s.

Enforcement policies include measures facilitating
paternity establishment, income withholding, and
the collection of child support payments.

Stronger enforcement increases the expected financial
responsibility of non-resident fathers and may therefore
alter incentives to have children outside marriage.

\paragraph{Data}

The main analysis uses US natality data from 1985 to 1998,
based on birth certificates.

Individual birth records are aggregated into cells
defined by state, year, maternal age, race, education,
and marital status.

The data provide information on the number of births,
maternal characteristics, prenatal care utilisation,
and infant birth weight.

The authors combine these records with state-level
child support enforcement expenditures, used as
a measure of enforcement intensity.

The analysis includes approximately 80,000
aggregated observations, depending on the outcome.

\paragraph{Identification Strategy}

The authors exploit variation in child support
enforcement expenditures across US states and over time.

A key challenge is that states with stronger enforcement
may also differ in economic conditions, family policies,
or social characteristics affecting fertility.

To address this issue, the authors compare changes
in outcomes among unmarried women relative to
married women within the same state and year.

Their empirical specification can be represented as:

\begin{equation}
Y_{gst}
=
\alpha_{st}
+
\beta
\left(
\ln(CSE_{st}) \times Single_g
\right)
+
\gamma Single_g
+
X_{gst}'\delta
+
\varepsilon_{gst},
\end{equation}

where $Y_{gst}$ denotes an outcome for demographic
group $g$ in state $s$ and year $t$.

$CSE_{st}$ measures state-level child support
enforcement expenditures, while $Single_g$
indicates unmarried mothers.

The term $\alpha_{st}$ represents state-by-year
fixed effects, which absorb shocks common to
married and unmarried women within each
state-year.

$X_{gst}$ includes demographic controls,
such as maternal age, education, and race.

The coefficient of interest, $\beta$, captures
the differential association between enforcement
intensity and outcomes for unmarried mothers,
relative to married mothers.

For fertility outcomes, the dependent variable
is the logarithm of the number of births.
For prenatal investments and birth outcomes,
the authors use measures of prenatal care
and low birth weight.

Identification relies on the assumption that
unobserved factors correlated with enforcement
do not differentially affect unmarried and
married women within the same state-year.

\paragraph{Main Results}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Nonmarital fertility:}
    The estimated coefficient on the interaction
    between log enforcement expenditures and
    unmarried status is $-0.033$ (Table 1).

    This implies that a 10\% increase in
    enforcement expenditures is associated
    with an approximately 0.33\% decline
    in births to unmarried women relative
    to married women.

    \item \textbf{Early prenatal care:}
    The estimated coefficient for initiation
    of prenatal care during the first trimester
    is $0.01847$ (Table 2).

    The authors estimate that increased
    enforcement accounts for approximately
    50\% of the observed improvement in
    early prenatal care among unmarried
    mothers over the study period.

    \item \textbf{Number of prenatal visits:}
    The estimated coefficient is $0.08071$
    (Table 2).

    The authors attribute approximately
    6\% of the observed increase in prenatal
    visits among unmarried mothers to
    stronger enforcement.

    \item \textbf{Low birth weight:}
    The estimated coefficient is $-0.00154$
    (Table 2).

    The authors estimate that stronger
    enforcement explains approximately
    13\% of the observed decline in
    low birth weight among children
    born to unmarried mothers.

    \item \textbf{Selection into parenthood:}
    The results are consistent with stronger
    enforcement changing the composition
    of nonmarital births, with relatively
    greater representation of more educated
    mothers and mothers with a higher
    propensity to invest in children.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Interpretation and Limitations}

The findings suggest that stronger child support
enforcement affects family outcomes through
both financial incentives and selection into
parenthood.

By increasing the expected cost of nonmarital
fatherhood, stricter enforcement may discourage
some nonmarital births.

Changes in the composition of parents may
subsequently influence prenatal investments
and infant health outcomes.

However, the estimated improvements in prenatal
care and birth outcomes cannot be interpreted
exclusively as behavioural changes among
a fixed population of mothers.

Part of the observed improvement may reflect
changes in which women become mothers.

Furthermore, enforcement intensity is measured
using state-level expenditures rather than
individual payment compliance.

The estimates therefore capture the effects
associated with broader enforcement intensity,
not the isolated effect of a specific
payment collection mechanism.

\paragraph{Relevance for Our Research Project}

This paper is particularly relevant to our
evaluation of France's 2022--2023 reform
introducing automatic intermediation
of child support payments.

Unlike studies focusing on changes in the
statutory amount of child support obligations,
Aizer and McLanahan investigate the effects
of stronger enforcement.

Their findings demonstrate that enforcement
policies may influence outcomes beyond
the financial transfers received by
custodial parents.

However, their analysis primarily concerns
fertility decisions, prenatal investments,
and selection into parenthood.

Our proposed project instead focuses on
families with existing child support obligations
and evaluates a reform changing the
administration and collection of payments.

Our contribution would therefore be to examine
whether automatic intermediation improves
actual payment compliance and the financial
security of creditor parents, while also
investigating potential behavioural responses
among debtor parents.

The paper highlights the importance of
distinguishing direct financial effects
from broader behavioural and compositional
effects of child support policies.

\section{Receiving Mothers: Labor Supply, Benefits, and Living Standards}

\subsection{C. Bonnet, B. Garbinti, A. Solaz, ``The Flip Side of Marital Specialization: The Gendered Effect of Divorce on Living Standards and Labor Supply'', Journal of Population Economics, 2021}

\paragraph{Motivation} Divorce hurts women more than men. In prior studies, women’s living standards fall by 10–40\%, while men’s stay stable or rise.
The usual explanation is \textbf{custody}: mothers bear the cost of raising the children. The authors argue this is only part of the story.
Theory (Cigno 2012; Lafortune \& Low 2019) treats marriage as a commitment device. It lets one spouse specialize in home production in exchange for compensation. If that compensation is insufficient at divorce, the cost of specialization shows up.
Existing evidence has three weaknesses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It mostly compares the same people before and after divorce, which is not causal.
    \item It relies on small survey samples with non-random attrition.
    \item It often observes only one side of child support (paid or received, not both).
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{Research question} What is the causal effect of divorce on each ex-spouse’s living standard and labor supply? And is the gender gap driven mainly by the number of children or by within-couple earnings inequality, used as a proxy for marital specialization?


\paragraph{Data and design} Data: exhaustive French income-tax and housing-tax returns. The sample covers couples who divorced or dissolved a PACS in 2009, aged 20–55: about 64k women and 56k men. \\

\noindent Outcome: living standard in 2008 vs 2010, built in three steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item pre-transfer income,
    \item plus child support,
    \item plus net public transfers (benefits are imputed).
\end{itemize}

\noindent Method: difference-in-differences combined with nearest-neighbor propensity score matching.
Matching is done separately in 24 groups defined by gender × number of children × the man’s share of couple earnings.
It uses more than 40 covariates, including earnings in each of the 3 years before divorce.

\paragraph{Contributions} \textbf{Data}: both ex-partners are observed, with child support both paid and received. Attrition is 7\% and is reweighted. \\
\noindent \textbf{Causal estimates}: women lose 19\% (vs –14\% before–after). For men, the sign flips to –2\% (vs +3.5\% before–after). \\
\noindent \textbf{Mechanism}: An accounting decomposition (eq. 1–2) and the regressions both show that within-couple earnings inequality matters far more than the number of children.
The second earner loses regardless of gender. Men who earned less than 40\% of couple earnings lose about 20\%. \\
\noindent \textbf{Role of transfers}: Public transfers remove the link between number of children and women’s losses.
Child support raises women’s living standard by about 6 percentage points and reduces men’s gain by about 9 points. \\
\noindent \textbf{Labor supply}: Previously inactive women are 29 percentage points more likely to enter work than their matched controls.
Working women’s labor income rises by 12\%. Divorced men face about a 40\% higher risk of unemployment.
Robustness: results hold for medians, alternative equivalence scales, PACS dissolutions and the repartnered sample. Repartnering largely offsets the losses.
